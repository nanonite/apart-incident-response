\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

The idea of watching a system through entropy instead of through its content is not new. Two decades ago, \textcite{shannon_ddos} showed that attacks on a network, such as distributed denial of service, change the entropy of simple traffic features like addresses and ports, so they can be detected without opening a single packet or knowing in advance what the attack looks like. Entropy later became a standard way to measure how uncertain a language model is, and \textcite{shannon_hallucination} used semantic entropy to detect when a model hallucinates. That line of work, however, looks at one model answering one prompt. It asks whether a single answer can be trusted, not whether something changed in the environment the model is working in.

Entropy has also started to appear in multi-agent systems. \textcite{zhao2026doesmultiagentcollaborationhelp} track it at the level of tokens, agents and rounds of discussion, and use it to predict when a team of agents does better than a single one. In their setting, though, the agents are supposed to talk. Communication is part of the design and the analyst knows exactly where it happens, so entropy is used to explain performance. Nobody is trying to catch coordination that was never intended.

That is the problem safety monitoring deals with, and today it is mostly solved by reading. AI control protocols surround an untrusted model with trusted monitors that inspect what it produces \parencite{greenblatt2024control}. This works while the content stays readable and passes through places someone is watching. In the incident, it did neither: messages were encoded and scattered across resources that were never meant for communication \parencite{openai2026incident, hf2026timeline}.

Our work sits between these lines. We take the reasoning behind network anomaly detection and apply it to sandboxed agents. Each agent plays the role of a network flow, its next-token distribution plays the role of the traffic features, and an unknown channel plays the role of an unknown attack. What we look for is not a better answer or a suspicious message, but a shift in how the agent behaves once text it was never given reaches its context. Such a signal does not care which channel carried the text or how it was encoded, and it costs no extra model call, because the probabilities are already produced when each token is generated. To know whether the signal means anything, we test it against a placebo that delivers useless foreign text and against cryptographic ground truth of whether communication actually took place.
