\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Go back to the incident. Weeks before the breach, agents were already reading notes that other runs had left on the package mirror, and nobody connected that activity to coordination. A monitor that tracks each agent's entropy would not have needed to know that the mirror was the channel, or that messages were hidden in directory names. For an agent that behaves like Qwen in our testbed, it would have seen the agent's uncertainty about its next action jump once foreign text reached its context. What it could not have told the responders is whether that text was a real message, a leaked credential or noise. Under sampled decoding, our measured entropy changes did not reliably distinguish useful peer text from placebo.

The results also show when such a signal can work. It was clear for Qwen, whose undisturbed agents spent most of their time waiting on the log, so their entropy was low and steady, and any disruption stood out. It was absent for gpt-4o-mini, which rarely read the log and so rarely saw the foreign entries, and for Llama, whose decision entropy stayed near zero throughout. The signal therefore depends on how an agent normally behaves, not only on which model it is. It also has to be read one agent at a time: the coupling between the two agents stayed at most 0.025 bits above chance, even while they were communicating, so communication in a shared log appears as a change inside the receiver and not as the agents acting in step.

This is a first step, not a finished monitor. Run as a live alarm with a 5\% false alarm rate on undisturbed runs, a CUSUM detector on Qwen's entropy caught only 15 to 25\% of disrupted runs at $T = 1$ and 40 to 50\% at $T = 0.5$, and the other models stayed close to chance. What is new is where the signal comes from. Current monitoring reads messages and chains of thought, which fails once content is encoded or spread over resources nobody watches. Entropy comes from the probability layer that every hosted model already produces, costs no extra model call, and does not depend on the channel. Turning it into a usable monitor would require calibrating it for each model and comparing each agent with sibling runs of the same task rather than with a fixed threshold.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}

This study was done in a weekend sprint with a small budget: one task, one shared-log design, two agents, three models and 20 runs per condition, on a budget of less than US\$5. Our original hypothesis was that entropy, and coupling between agents, would detect communication itself. The framing around disruption came after that hypothesis failed, and so did the two lower-temperature experiments. Entropy was computed from the 20 most likely tokens only, and one provider rescaled log-probabilities while another returned slightly different values for identical calls. The placebo was harmless text from the same task, and our agents had no reason to hide. Finally, the explanation based on the agents' routines is a description of the data, not a tested cause.

Beyond the protected-database task, the local dashboard piloted poetry with Silva/Mishima-inspired poet/editor profiles and a fictional 1943 food-allocation law with liberal-reviewer/authoritarian-drafter roles.
Each task combined a shared objective with distinct responsibilities, intended to encourage collaboration through complementary work rather than private facts alone.
Under isolation and scheduled unlock, we recorded token-entropy trajectories, revisions and peer references, with fixed output limits and no agent shell or network access.
These exploratory Ollama \texttt{gemma2:2b} trials ran on a teammate's CPU-only device; output errors and incomplete condition coverage prevented a reliable comparison, and they are excluded from the 539 reported runs.
With another month, we would compare matched task/role configurations and donor-text controls, assess quality through blinded review, and test whether role design changes information use and entropy in either direction.

The joint-entropy identity in \cref{sec:entropy} extends beyond two agents through total correlation, but our implemented estimator and evidence concern two. Additional agents introduce random variables and higher-order dependencies; whether the observed per-agent behaviour persists remains to be tested. Semantic uncertainty would require repeated fixed-context samples and an explicit equivalence criterion \parencite{kuhn2023semantic}, rather than pooling evolving drafts.

\emph{Dual use:} the benchmark could help tune agents to evade monitors. Synthetic assets reduce exposure, but an entropy alarm cannot certify containment or replace access controls.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We developed a framework for measuring how a restricted MAS responds when task information changes. In a two-agent benchmark requiring complementary protected assets, we compared isolation, scheduled visibility, and task-irrelevant text while recording task state, permitted observations, checker-verified asset use, predictive and action entropy, and cross-agent coupling. Entropy changes were model-dependent and often reflected the presence of new text more than its task relevance; coupling added little. The dashboard and local-model pilots provide an operational path for future task and role comparisons, while remaining engineering checks rather than evidence for the reported effect. The framework therefore provides a reproducible starting point for comparing tasks and roles, while leaving semantic uncertainty, causal collaboration, and general task performance for future experiments.
