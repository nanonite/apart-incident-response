\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the results mean.} The incident reports describe signals that existed weeks before the breach but were not recognised as coordination \parencite{openai2026incident}. Our results suggest what an entropy monitor could and could not have added. For a model like Qwen3 235B, and if our setting transfers, it would have flagged runs whose agents started reading content they were not given, from the log-probabilities the operator already had, without knowing that the package mirror was the channel. It would not have said whether that content was a partner's message, a scraped web page or noise, and it would have fired on all of them. That is still useful as triage: in a fleet of thousands of runs, a cheap signal that points reviewers at the runs whose information environment changed addresses the gap the reports describe. The same property makes it relevant beyond collusion, since indirect prompt injection is also foreign text arriving in context \parencite{greshake2023indirect}.

Three trends matter for anyone building on this. First, detectability is a property of the model: the same disruption moved Qwen's entropy strongly, Llama's weakly and gpt-4o-mini's not at all. Second, lower temperature helped. At $T = 0$ undisturbed runs become nearly deterministic, so any change stands out, and it was the only setting where real information and placebo text were separable. Third, the simplest per-agent statistic did better than the system-level decomposition that motivated the project: coupling between agents stayed within 0.025 bits of chance.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The disruption framing was chosen after the experiments, when the communication hypothesis failed. The conditions and the per-condition tests were fixed beforehand, but the pooled disrupted-against-base test and the two lower-temperature experiments were added afterwards, and readers should weigh them accordingly. We tested one task, one shared-resource design, one switch turn and two agents, with 20 runs per cell. Positive results come mainly from one model, and the strongest greedy result rests on runs that were partly identical. Entropy is computed from the top 20 tokens, and one provider's log-probabilities varied between identical calls. The placebo was benign text from the same task family; foreign text that closely mimics an agent's own entries, or content an adversary shapes to keep entropy flat, may not be detected. Our agents did not know they were monitored and had no reason to hide.

Several assumptions shape the interpretation. We assume that providers return log-probabilities of the model that produced the output; if a provider altered or rescaled them, as Vertex did for temperature, the monitor measures the provider. We assume that the pre-switch window is a valid baseline for each run; in long-running agents with no clean baseline, the monitor would need a reference from comparable runs instead. We assume that disruption shows up within a few turns; slow, low-volume channels such as the months-long board in the incident could stay below a CUSUM threshold. Finally, the monitor needs log-probability access, so it is available to the operator hosting the agents, not to the victim.

\paragraph{Future work.} The next steps are to test more models, including open-weight models served locally where full-vocabulary entropy is available; to vary the kind of foreign content (partner messages, prompt injections, retrieved documents) to see whether they leave different entropy signatures; to test a monitor that compares an agent with its sibling runs rather than with its own past, which is closer to how parallel evaluations are run; to scale beyond two agents, where total correlation \parencite{watanabe1960} may behave differently from pairwise coupling; and to test agents that know about the monitor and are rewarded for evading it.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We built a two-agent testbed with verified communication and a matched placebo, ran it with three models at three temperatures, and asked whether output entropy reveals that foreign content entered an agent's context. It does for Qwen3 235B, with AUC up to 0.96 from one run-level statistic and a result that replicated at every temperature. It does weakly for Llama 3.3 70B and not for gpt-4o-mini. Under sampling, entropy responded as strongly to useless foreign text as to real messages, and only greedy decoding let one model separate the two. The two-agent coupling term added almost nothing.

Entropy is therefore a candidate for a cheap, channel-agnostic disruption monitor that tells reviewers where to look, not a communication detector. It needs per-model validation, and it should be tested against content designed to evade it before anyone relies on it.
