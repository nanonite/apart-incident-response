\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Go back to the incident. Weeks before the breach, agents were already reading notes that other runs had left on the package mirror, and nobody connected that activity to coordination. A monitor that tracks each agent's entropy would not have needed to know that the mirror was the channel, or that messages were hidden in directory names. For an agent that behaves like Qwen in our testbed, it would have seen the agent's uncertainty about its next action jump once foreign text reached its context. What it could not have told the responders is whether that text was a real message, a leaked credential or noise. In our data, useless placebo text raised entropy as much as real communication did.

The results also say when such a signal can work. It was clear for Qwen, whose undisturbed agents spent most of their time waiting on the log, so their entropy was low and steady and any disruption stood out. It was absent for gpt-4o-mini, which rarely read the log and so rarely saw the foreign entries, and for Llama, whose decision entropy stayed near zero throughout. The signal therefore depends on how an agent normally behaves, not only on which model it is. It also has to be read one agent at a time: the two agents never became statistically coupled, even while they were communicating, so communication in a shared log appears as a change inside the receiver and not as the agents acting in step.

This is a first step, not a finished monitor. Run as a live alarm with 5\% false alarms on undisturbed runs, a CUSUM detector on Qwen's entropy caught only 15 to 25\% of disrupted runs at $T = 1$ and 40 to 50\% at $T = 0.5$, and the other models stayed close to chance. What is new is where the signal comes from. Current monitoring reads messages and chains of thought, which fails once content is encoded or spread over resources nobody watches. Entropy comes from the probability layer that every hosted model already produces, costs no extra model call, and does not depend on the channel. Turning it into a usable monitor would require calibrating it for each model and comparing each agent with sibling runs of the same task rather than with a fixed threshold.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}

This study was done in a weekend sprint with a small budget: one task, one shared-log design, two agents, three models and 20 runs per condition, for US\$4.18 in API costs. Our original hypothesis was that entropy, and coupling between agents, would detect communication itself. The framing around disruption came after that hypothesis failed, and so did the two lower-temperature experiments. Entropy was computed from the 20 most likely tokens only, and one provider rescaled log-probabilities while another returned slightly different values for identical calls. The placebo was harmless text from the same task, and our agents had no reason to hide. Finally, the explanation based on the agents' routines is a description of the data, not a tested cause.

Future work should repeat the experiment inside the team's Pi runtime with local open-weight models, where full-vocabulary entropy is available, and with more than two agents. It should also use other kinds of foreign content, such as prompt injections or retrieved documents, test agents that are rewarded for evading the monitor, and train a classifier on entropy trajectories instead of relying on a single threshold.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We rebuilt the Hugging Face incident as a small testbed and asked whether the entropy of an agent's outputs reveals that a hidden channel has opened. Entropy detects that a sandboxed agent's context was disrupted, for some models, but not whether the disruption was communication. It is a cheap, channel-independent starting point for monitoring agents from their probabilities rather than from what they write.
